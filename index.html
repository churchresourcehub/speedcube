Set up to improve

Three things hold back a two-minute solver before technique even matters: the cube, the lack of a clock, and not being able to read algorithms.

Get a speedcube

If you are still on a Rubik's brand cube or a stiff one, replace it first. A modern magnetic speedcube costs $10 to $20, turns with one finger, and cuts corners so a slightly misaligned turn does not jam. Budget magnetic cubes from MoYu, QiYi and GAN are all fine. Nothing in Level 1 works on a cube you have to grip and wrench.

  • Magnets pull each layer into line so turns finish themselves.
  • Tension. If the cube pops or locks, tighten or loosen the centre screws a quarter turn at a time.
  • Lube. A drop of silicone cube lube every few weeks keeps it smooth. Optional at first.

Learn to read notation

Every algorithm from here on is written in letters. Each letter is a face, turned a quarter turn clockwise as if you were looking straight at that face. Tap the moves below and watch what each one does.

R L U D F BRight, Left, Up, Down, Front, Back. One clockwise quarter turn.
R'The apostrophe means counter-clockwise. Said "R prime".
R2Turn it twice.
r f uLower case means two layers together: the face plus the middle slice next to it.
MThe middle slice between L and R, turned the same way as L.
x y zTurn the whole cube: x like R, y like U, z like F.
( )Brackets group moves your hands do as one motion. They do not change the moves.

Fix your hands and your habits

No new method yet. The same solve you know, done with better turning and fewer wasted motions, is worth 30 to 45 seconds. These habits also carry through every later level, so they are worth getting right before you add algorithms on top.

Finger tricks

Beginners turn each face with the whole hand and re-grip between every move. Fast solvers hold the cube still and flick.

Watch the two triggers

Nearly every algorithm in this course is built from these two four-move pieces. Play each one and copy it on your cube.

Cross on the bottom

If you build a daisy on top and then flip each petal down, or build the cross on top and turn the cube over, stop. Solve the white cross directly on the bottom face while you look at the sides. It feels blind at first. After a week you gain a full view of the top two layers for the rest of the solve, which is what F2L needs.

  • Hold white on the bottom from the first move to the last. No more flipping the cube over.
  • Before you start, find all four white edges. Solve them in an order that does not knock an earlier one out.
  • Place edges relative to each other. If blue and red are correct next to each other, one final D turn lines up all four at once.

Stop rotating the cube

Every time you spin the cube to "check the back", you lose a second and lose track of the pieces. Pick a front face and keep it. Use a y turn only when a piece truly cannot be reached.

Common mistakes at this level

  • Drilling fast and sloppy. Slow and clean builds the habit; speed arrives by itself.
  • Letting go of the cube with the right hand to turn U. The index finger does it.
  • Looking at the white face while building the cross. Look at the side colours.

F2L: solve corners and edges in pairs

This is the biggest change in the whole course. Right now you place four white corners, then four middle edges: eight steps. F2L joins each corner to its edge in the top layer and inserts them together: four steps, and far fewer moves in each. About half of every fast solve is F2L, so this is where most of your time lives from now on.

Learn it by understanding, not by memorising. There are 41 possible cases and you do not need a list. You need two ideas.

  1. Get both pieces into the top layer. If one is stuck in a slot, lift it out with R U R' or R U' R'.
  2. Hide one, move the other, and bring them together. Then insert the pair with one of the four basic inserts below.

The four basic inserts, and two harder cases

Every F2L case ends in one of the first four. Tap a card to watch it play. Yellow is on top, grey pieces do not matter yet, and the slot being filled is the front-right one between green and orange.

The last two cards are examples of the "split and re-join" idea. Step through them one move at a time and work out why they work. That understanding is the skill; the move sequence is only its result.

How to think through any pair

  1. Find a corner with white on it in the top layer. Read its other two colours.
  2. Find the edge with those same two colours.
  3. Ask: are they already joined correctly? Then insert. Are they joined wrongly, or apart? Then move one out of the way with the slot's face, turn the top, and bring it back.
  4. Before you insert, look away from the pair and start hunting the next one.

How to practise it

  • Week 1, untimed. Solve the cross, then solve each pair slowly, talking yourself through it. Solves will take three or four minutes. That is expected.
  • Week 2. Still untimed, but stop using your old corner-then-edge method entirely, even when a case stumps you.
  • Weeks 3 and 4. Bring the timer back. Finish the last layer with what you already know.
  • Use whichever slot is easiest for the pair you find. You do not have to fill the front-right one.

Common mistakes at this level

  • Going back to the old method when the clock is running. Leave the timer off for two weeks.
  • Memorising a move list for each case. If you cannot say why it works, slow down.
  • Rotating the cube for every pair. Learn the left-hand inserts early.

Two-look last layer

The beginner last layer repeats one or two algorithms many times. Two-look OLL and two-look PLL finish the top in at most four short algorithms. You learn 16 in total, and several you already know under other names. Tap any card to watch it and mark how well you know it.

OLL look 1: make the yellow cross

Look only at the four top edges. Diagrams are the top face seen from above; the thin bars show yellow stickers facing sideways.

OLL look 2: turn the corners yellow

With the cross made, count the yellow corners on top. One means Sune or Antisune. None means H or Pi. Two means Headlights, Chameleon or Bowtie. Turn the top until it matches the picture, then go.

PLL look 1: corners

Now the top is yellow. Look at the side colours of the corners. Find a face where both corners match each other ("headlights"), put it on the left, and do the T perm. If no face has headlights, do the Y perm from anywhere.

PLL look 2: edges

With corners solved, one of four edge cases is left. If one side is a complete bar, hold it at the back and use Ua or Ub.

Drill these 16 in the trainer

How to learn an algorithm so it sticks

  • Learn one a day, two at most. Start with the ones built from R U R' U'.
  • Break it into chunks of three or four moves, following the brackets. Say the chunks, not the letters.
  • Do it 20 times slowly while watching what the pieces do. Then 20 times without looking.
  • Next day, do it cold before anything else. If it is gone, it was never in your hands, only in your head. Repeat the 20.
  • To set up a case for practice, do the algorithm backwards on a solved cube.

Plan the whole cross before you start

In competition you get 15 seconds to inspect the cube before the clock starts. Use the same rule at home. Plan two cross edges, then three, then all four, and solve them with your eyes closed to prove the plan was complete. Any cross can be done in 8 moves or fewer; aim to average under 3 seconds.

Look-ahead: slow down to get faster

Film one solve. You will see bursts of fast turning with dead pauses between them while you hunt for the next pair. The pauses are most of your time. The fix is to turn slowly enough that your eyes are free: while your hands insert one pair, your eyes are already finding the next. A steady solve at half speed with no pauses beats a fast one with stops.

  • Never watch the pair you are inserting. You already know where it is going.
  • Learn to insert into the back slots without rotating, using L U L', R' U' R and their joined-pair versions.
  • Solve pairs into the back first when you can, so the pieces left over are in front where you can see them.

Level 50:25 → 0:182 to 4 months

Full OLL and efficient F2L

This is where most people who stick with it break 20 seconds. The work is steadier and less dramatic than the earlier levels.

Full OLL: 57 algorithms, one look

You know ten. The other 47 remove the second OLL look, saving two to three seconds a solve. Learn them in groups by shape, two or three a week, and keep using two-look for the ones you have not reached. Many are a known algorithm with one setup move.

All 57 are in the Algorithms screen, grouped by shape, each with a player. Work through the groups in this order.

  1. Corners Oriented and Cross. You already know most of these from two-look.
  2. T, P and Square shapes. Short, and built from triggers you have.
  3. Fish, W, Knight Move and L shapes.
  4. Lightning Bolts, C, I and Awkward shapes.
  5. Dot cases last. They are the rarest.

Fix your worst F2L cases

Your intuitive solutions for some cases are 11 or 12 moves when an 8-move one exists. Go through the full case list once, compare each to what you do, and replace only the ones where yours is clearly longer or needs a rotation.

Cross into first pair

Once the cross plan takes five seconds of your fifteen, spend the rest tracking one corner or edge of your first pair through the cross moves. You then start F2L with no pause at all.

Drill: film and count

Once a week, film five solves. For each one, count the cube rotations, count the pauses longer than half a second, and use split mode in the timer for each stage. Pick the single worst number and make it the focus of the next week.

Advanced techniques

Under 20 seconds you have learned everything that is required. What follows is optional, each item worth a few tenths to a second. Pick them up one at a time, in roughly this order. Ten-second averages come from doing all of this for thousands of solves, and plenty of dedicated cubers settle happily at 12 to 15.

  • X-cross. Solve the cross and the first pair together during inspection, so F2L starts with three pairs left.
  • Multislotting and keyhole. Use the moves of one insert to set up the next pair.
  • Edge control. Change your last pair insert so the top edges come out already oriented, giving you one of the seven easy OLLs.
  • COLL. About 40 algorithms that solve the corners completely while orienting, leaving only an easy edge PLL.
  • Winter Variation and ZBLL. Hundreds of algorithms for one-look last layers. Only worth it once you are well under 12.
  • Turning speed. Now, and only now, raw speed drills matter: time your PLLs one by one in the trainer and aim for each under 1.5 seconds.
  • A competition. Nothing sharpens practice like a date on the calendar. All ages and speeds enter; a 25-second average is welcome.
